Before trusting any rating on any shop, know how the sausage is made. Everything below was found while researching these exact watches; the receipts are in the research documents.

## Free products buy good reviews

Czech platform **Testuj.to** runs campaigns where ~10 testers receive a watch free in exchange for obligations that include posting reviews. Those reviews then appear on retail sites as ordinary customer opinions. Several watches in our table have review pools visibly shaped by such campaigns.

## Seeded comment sections

On one watch's YouTube review, **19 of 76 comments** came from a single coordinated account cluster praising the product. That claim was withdrawn once during our audits for lacking receipts — then reinstated when a deep pass produced them. Both states are left visible in the research document, which is how every claim on this site is meant to work.

## Star ratings depend on which country asks

The same app can show a different score in each country's Play Store, and Romania's is sometimes not shown at all. A "4.0★ app" screenshot proves little; the research documents quote ratings with the storefront and date attached.

## Marketplace review farming

On eMAG and Amazon alike, review pools get contaminated: reviews for different models merged into one score, incentivised five-star bursts, and seller-switching under one listing. The research documents quarantine contaminated pools and count only model-verified owner reports.

## What to do instead

Ignore the star number. Read the worst reviews for the failure *mechanism* (does it die indoors? does the SOS false-fire?), check the date and the country, and weight a dated first-hand report on a named network above everything else. That is all this project's method really is.
